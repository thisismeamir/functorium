To begin with Quantum Mechanics it is important to understand why is was necessary in the first place. In the early 20th century it was observed that classical mechanics, with all of its capabilities to explain, and predict a vast range of phenomena, failed to do so in some.

This observation led to development of ad-hoc theories, which shaped the [[Early Form of Quantum Mechanics]]. Later it was formalized into a solid theory [[Standard Approach to Quantum Mechanics|Standard Quantum Mechanics]].

In this document we would examine the stage that quantum mechanic rose from. Namely, the historical overview of the late 19th and early 20th century of physics. 

“Alfred North Whitehead once referred to the last quarter of the nineteenth century as “an age of successful scientific orthodoxy, undisturbed by much thought beyond the conventions. . . . one of the dullest stages of thought since the time of the First Crusade” (Whitehead 1925, 148).” (Kragh, 2002, p. 3)[^2]

“Albert Michelson, the great American experimentalist, said in 1894 that “it seems probable that most of the grand underlying principles have been firmly established and that further advances are to be sought chiefly in the rigorous application of these principles to all the phenomena which come under our notice” (Badash 1972, 52).” (Kragh, 2002, p. 3)[^2]

How much this view about physics was widespread it to be in question, but what is known that it held truth to it. It wasn't expected that anything interesting is going to happen to physics in the next century, mainly because classical mechanics was so good at explaining phenomena that experimentalists were encountering. And up until a point, it was only a matter of more and more precise measurements.

According to the classical mechanical world picture (The Laplacian version of Newtonianism), the world consisted of atoms, which were the sites of, and on which acted, various forces of long and short ranges.

“With the advent of field theory, the mechanism of force propagation changed, but Maxwell and most other field physicists continued to seek a mechanical foundation for their models.” (Kragh, 2002, p. 4)[^2]

“The most important conceptual shift was perhaps the rise to prominence indeed, necessity of a universal ether as the quasihypothetical, continuous, and all-pervading medium through which forces propagated with a finite speed.” (Kragh, 2002, p. 4)[^2]
