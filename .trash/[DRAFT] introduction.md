## Future

### Ground-Based Experiments

The era of ground-based gravitational-wave detection with LIGO and Virgo is ongoing, with further instrumental improvements already planned. One such upgrade is Advanced LIGO Plus $A+$, which aims to improve broadband sensitivity through the use of quantum light squeezing. This technique reduces phase noise at high frequencies and radiation-pressure noise at low frequencies, thereby enhancing the overall detector performance.

Gravitational-wave astronomy is not limited to the LIGO and Virgo observatories. Japan has constructed a $3$-km underground interferometer, KAGRA, located in the Kamioka mine. This detector represents the first implementation of cryogenic interferometric detection. KAGRA is intended to operate as part of the global gravitational-wave detector network alongside LIGO and Virgo. In addition, the LIGO-India detector is expected to become operational in the mid-2020s. With detectors distributed globally, the network will achieve improved sensitivity to gravitational waves arriving from different directions and will operate closer to optimal performance.

Although predictions beyond 2025 are uncertain, plans for more advanced ground-based detectors are already under discussion. Two major proposals are the Einstein Telescope and the Cosmic Explorer. Both concepts require new facilities. The Einstein Telescope is planned as an underground triangular interferometer to mitigate seismic noise, while the Cosmic Explorer envisions a detector with arm lengths of approximately $40$ km, incorporating quantum squeezing techniques similar to those of $A+$ and cryogenic cooling technologies developed for KAGRA and Voyager. At present, funding for these projects has not been secured, and only early-phase studies have begun. Consequently, deployment is not expected before the 2030s.

The increased sensitivity of these future detectors is expected to mark a transition to a precision era of gravitational-wave astronomy. Lower noise levels will result in significantly higher event detection rates, potentially reaching thousands of events depending on astrophysical source populations. These detections will include moderate signal-to-noise ratio events from sources at distances of thousands of megaparsecs, as well as a smaller number of nearby events with very high signal-to-noise ratios, potentially in the hundreds.

It is anticipated that the improved sensitivity of next-generation ground-based detectors will both enhance measurement precision and lead to unexpected discoveries.

### Cosmic Gravitational-Wave Detection

In addition to the frequency range of approximately $10–2,000 \text{Hz}$ probed by current and planned ground-based detectors, lower-frequency gravitational waves can be studied indirectly through cosmic phenomena. These approaches provide access to gravitational-wave signals outside the reach of terrestrial interferometers.

### Space-Based Observations

To detect gravitational waves emitted by different classes of strong-gravity sources, space-based detectors are required. One such mission is the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA), a mission led by the European Space Agency with participation from NASA. LISA will consist of three spacecraft arranged in a triangular formation, orbiting the Sun while trailing the Earth and maintaining a precessing configuration.

LISA is expected to detect new classes of gravitational-wave sources, including mergers of black holes with masses in the range $10^4 M_\odot$ to $10^7 M_\odot$ located at galactic centers. The gravitational waves emitted by these systems contain information about the properties of black holes prior to and during galaxy mergers, contributing to astrophysical population studies.

Space-based gravitational-wave observations are particularly important due to the unique science they enable. Information about supermassive black holes during galaxy mergers can only be obtained through these observations. From a theoretical perspective, gravitational waves emitted in extreme-mass-ratio inspirals encode detailed information about the spacetime geometry of supermassive black holes.

Such measurements allow for tests of whether astrophysical black holes are described by the Kerr solution of the Einstein equations. Previous experience suggests that these observations are likely to confirm many predictions of general relativity, rule out certain modified gravity models, and constrain models of black hole formation. However, the possibility of unexpected results remains.

Historically, opening new observational windows has led to unforeseen discoveries. Gravitational-wave observations may provide insights into unresolved questions in gravitational physics, including the late-time acceleration of the Universe, galactic rotation curves, and the unification of quantum mechanics with gravity.

## 1.1 Motivation and Scope

The direct detection of gravitational waves by the LIGO and Virgo collaborations in 2015 marked a watershed moment in physics, opening an entirely new window onto the universe. What Einstein predicted a century ago as minute ripples in the fabric of spacetime has become a powerful observational tool, fundamentally transforming our understanding of compact objects, extreme gravity, and the cosmos itself. In less than a decade since that first detection, gravitational wave astronomy has evolved from a bold experimental venture into a mature observational science, with over 90 confirmed detections and counting.

This feasibility study examines the current state and future directions of gravitational wave physics, with particular emphasis on the computational and numerical challenges that lie at the heart of extracting physical insight from these observations. The study serves three primary objectives: first, to establish why gravitational wave research remains a vital and active frontier of fundamental physics; second, to demonstrate the field's current relevance through recent advances and ongoing developments; and third, to identify concrete research directions—specifically those involving numerical relativity and data analysis—that could form the basis of a master's thesis.

Gravitational wave physics sits at a unique intersection of theoretical physics, computational science, and experimental observation. Understanding the signals requires solving Einstein's field equations in their full nonlinear glory—a task only achievable through large-scale numerical simulations. Extracting astrophysical information from detector data demands sophisticated data analysis techniques, increasingly augmented by machine learning. Planning for the next generation of more sensitive detectors requires anticipating computational challenges that scale with improved sensitivity. This computational intensity is not merely a practical consideration; it defines many of the field's open questions and opportunities for contribution.

The scope of this study is deliberately comprehensive. Rather than narrowly focusing on a single subfield, we survey the landscape from fundamental tests of general relativity through waveform modeling, data analysis methodologies, current and future observational facilities, and emerging computational techniques. This breadth serves a purpose: identifying where different aspects of the field intersect often reveals the most promising research opportunities. A challenge in numerical relativity may have immediate implications for data analysis; a limitation in current waveform models may be addressable through novel computational approaches; an anticipated capability of future detectors may require computational infrastructure that doesn't yet exist.

The structure of this study is organized around fundamental scientific questions rather than subdisciplinary boundaries. This approach reflects how gravitational wave research actually proceeds—theoretical predictions must be validated computationally, computational results must be tested against observations, and observational capabilities determine which theoretical questions can be addressed. Each major section examines a core question in the field: What can gravitational waves tell us about gravity itself? What can we learn about compact objects and their evolution? How do we maximize the information extracted from observations? What does the future hold for gravitational wave astronomy? For each question, we trace the connections between theoretical understanding, computational challenges, and observational prospects, explicitly highlighting open problems and opportunities for contribution.

This study arrives at a particularly opportune moment. The current generation of ground-based detectors is entering a phase of regular observations with steadily improving sensitivity. The LISA space-based mission was formally adopted in January 2024 and enters its construction phase, promising to open the millihertz frequency band by the mid-2030s. Next-generation ground-based facilities—Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer—are moving from conceptual design toward realization, with site selection and detailed planning underway. Machine learning techniques are moving from exploratory applications to operational deployment in detection and analysis pipelines. Numerical relativity is pushing toward the accuracy requirements these future detectors will demand. The field is simultaneously consolidating the gains of the first detection era while preparing for qualitatively new capabilities on the horizon.

For a researcher entering the field now, this timing creates exceptional opportunities. The first decade of detections has identified clear challenges: we need more accurate waveform models for parameter estimation with next-generation detectors; we need faster analysis methods to handle increasing detection rates; we need to understand systematic errors that could bias tests of general relativity; we need computational tools that can model systems we haven't yet simulated with sufficient accuracy. These are not abstract concerns—they are concrete problems with defined goals, existing resources, and active communities working toward solutions. They are, in other words, excellent thesis topics.

This study is also motivated by practical considerations. Gravitational wave research requires substantial computational resources, but these are increasingly accessible through collaborations, shared facilities, and open-source software infrastructure. The field has embraced open data and reproducible research, with the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center providing access to detector data and analysis tutorials. Major numerical relativity codes are open source and actively maintained. Waveform catalogs and data analysis tools are publicly available. The barrier to entry for contributing meaningful work has never been lower, even as the scientific questions remain profound.

Finally, this study recognizes that choosing a thesis direction requires more than identifying important problems—it requires finding problems that align with personal interests, available resources, and the expertise of one's research group. The goal is not merely to survey what is known, but to identify what gaps exist, what tools are available to address them, and what kind of research would be both scientifically valuable and personally engaging. Throughout this document, we pay particular attention to computational challenges and opportunities, reflecting the conviction that advances in simulation and analysis capabilities will drive much of the field's progress over the coming years.

The gravitational wave revolution is far from over. In many ways, it has only just begun.

## 1.2 The Gravitational Wave Revolution: A Brief History

### The Theoretical Foundation (1916-1970s)

Gravitational waves emerged from Einstein's general theory of relativity in 1916, just months after he formulated the field equations. In the linearized weak-field limit, Einstein showed that gravitational disturbances propagate through spacetime at the speed of light, carrying energy away from accelerating masses. However, the physical reality of these waves remained controversial for decades. Even Einstein himself wavered, publishing a paper in 1936 claiming gravitational waves didn't exist (he later retracted this after errors were identified). The question of whether gravitational waves were mathematical artifacts of coordinate choice or physically observable phenomena wasn't definitively settled until the 1950s through the work of Bondi, Pirani, and others.

The theoretical understanding of gravitational radiation accelerated in the 1960s. The quadrupole formula, describing the leading-order emission from slowly moving sources, provided the foundation for calculating energy loss from orbiting systems. Post-Newtonian approximations extended these calculations to higher orders, enabling predictions for how binary systems should evolve as they radiate energy. By the early 1970s, theorists had a clear picture of what gravitational waves should do—they just hadn't been detected.

### The First Indirect Evidence (1974-1993)

The 1974 discovery of the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16 by Hulse and Taylor provided the first indirect evidence for gravitational radiation. This system—two neutron stars in a tight orbit—offered a natural laboratory for testing general relativity's predictions. As the system radiates gravitational waves, it should lose orbital energy, causing the orbital period to decrease at a precisely calculable rate. Over the following years, radio timing observations tracked this orbital decay with extraordinary precision. By 1993, when Hulse and Taylor received the Nobel Prize, the observed decay matched general relativity's predictions to better than 0.5%. Gravitational waves were real; they just hadn't been directly detected.

### Building the Detectors (1970s-2015)

The quest for direct detection began in the 1960s with Joseph Weber's resonant bar detectors. Though his claimed detections were never confirmed, Weber's pioneering work inspired a generation of experimental physicists. By the 1970s, attention had shifted to interferometric detectors, which promised far greater sensitivity. The concept was straightforward: split a laser beam, send the two parts down perpendicular arms several kilometers long, recombine them, and look for tiny phase shifts caused by passing gravitational waves. The execution, however, required decades of technological development.

The LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) project was proposed in the 1980s and approved by the U.S. Congress in 1992. Construction of two 4-kilometer facilities—one in Hanford, Washington, and one in Livingston, Louisiana—was completed in 1999. Initial LIGO operated from 2002 to 2010 without detecting gravitational waves, as expected; the technology wasn't yet sensitive enough. The crucial upgrade to Advanced LIGO, incorporating decades of technical improvements, was completed in 2015.

Europe built the Virgo detector near Pisa, Italy, with 3-kilometer arms, which began operations in 2007 and underwent similar sensitivity upgrades. Japan constructed KAGRA, an underground 3-kilometer detector in the Kamioka mine, which joined the network in 2020. This global network of detectors is essential for localizing sources on the sky and testing the polarization properties of gravitational waves.

### First Detection and the New Astronomy (2015-Present)

On September 14, 2015, at 09:50:45 UTC, Advanced LIGO detected gravitational waves from the merger of two black holes, each roughly 30 times the mass of the Sun, located about 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal—designated GW150914—swept through LIGO's sensitive frequency band in a tenth of a second, matching theoretical predictions from numerical relativity simulations with remarkable fidelity. The waveform's shape encoded the masses and spins of the black holes, the distance to the system, and even confirmed that the merged remnant obeyed general relativity's predictions for black hole ringdown. This single detection accomplished multiple scientific goals simultaneously: it confirmed gravitational waves exist, proved that binary black holes exist and merge within a Hubble time, and tested general relativity in the strong-field regime for the first time.

The announcement in February 2016 was met with worldwide acclaim. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, and Kip Thorne for their leadership roles in LIGO. But the scientific revolution was just beginning.

The truly transformative event came on August 17, 2017, with the detection of GW170817—gravitational waves from a binary neutron star merger. Within seconds of the gravitational wave detection, the Fermi and INTEGRAL gamma-ray satellites detected a short gamma-ray burst from the same sky region. Within hours, optical telescopes located the host galaxy (NGC 4993) and observed the kilonova—the radioactive glow from newly synthesized heavy elements. Over the following weeks, telescopes across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio to X-rays, tracked the event's evolution. This single event spawned over 100 papers in the first few months, addressing questions ranging from the neutron star equation of state to the cosmic origin of gold and platinum, from the Hubble constant to tests of fundamental physics.

GW170817 inaugurated the era of multi-messenger astronomy with gravitational waves. It demonstrated that gravitational wave observations could guide electromagnetic follow-up, that joint observations could constrain astrophysics and cosmology in ways neither could alone, and that the gravitational wave community could coordinate with observers worldwide to maximize scientific return. The event also validated decades of theoretical work on neutron star mergers and showed that these systems are indeed factories for heavy element production.

### The Current Era: Regular Observations

Following GW170817, the pace of discoveries accelerated. The first two observing runs (O1 and O2) detected 11 gravitational wave events. The third observing run (O3), split into O3a and O3b from April 2019 to March 2020, detected approximately 80 additional events. The third Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog (GWTC-3), released in 2021, included 90 confident detections. These observations have revealed:

- **A diverse population of binary black holes**, ranging from stellar-mass systems to intermediate-mass mergers, with a wide range of mass ratios and spin configurations. The population properties provide constraints on formation channels, stellar evolution, and binary dynamics.
    
- **Binary neutron star systems** beyond GW170817, though none with confirmed electromagnetic counterparts. These detections constrain the neutron star mass distribution and equation of state.
    
- **Neutron star-black hole binaries**, confirming that this channel contributes to the merger rate and enabling new tests of compact object physics.
    
- **Evidence for hierarchical mergers**—black holes that themselves resulted from previous mergers—suggesting dense stellar environments where repeated interactions can build up increasingly massive black holes.
    
- **Tests of general relativity** with increasing precision. So far, all observations are consistent with GR predictions, placing stringent constraints on alternative theories of gravity.
    

The field has matured to the point where multiple detections per week are routine during observing runs. The fourth observing run (O4) began in May 2023 with improved sensitivity across the network. The Japanese KAGRA detector joined at reduced sensitivity, working toward full integration. Plans for O5, potentially beginning in 2026-2027, promise further sensitivity improvements and higher detection rates.

### Looking Ahead: The Next Decades

The gravitational wave revolution is entering its second phase. The current detector network will continue to improve, eventually reaching design sensitivity and possibly being upgraded further. But the most dramatic developments will come from new facilities:

- **LISA (Laser Interferometer Space Antenna)**, formally adopted by ESA in January 2024, will launch in the mid-2030s to observe millihertz-frequency gravitational waves from space. LISA will detect supermassive black hole mergers, extreme mass ratio inspirals, and potentially stochastic backgrounds from the early universe—sources completely inaccessible to ground-based detectors due to seismic noise.
    
- **Einstein Telescope**, a proposed European underground facility with 10-kilometer arms, could improve sensitivity by a factor of 10 over current detectors. Site selection is underway, with the Euregio Meuse-Rhine region (spanning Belgium, Netherlands, and Germany) and Sardinia, Italy, as leading candidates.
    
- **Cosmic Explorer**, a U.S. proposal for two facilities with 40-kilometer and 20-kilometer arms, would achieve similar sensitivity improvements. The longer arms enable detection of binary black holes across cosmic time, potentially observing the first generation of black holes formed in the universe.
    

These next-generation facilities will not merely detect more events—they will enable qualitatively new science. With sensitivities 10 times better than current detectors, they will observe every stellar-mass binary black hole merger in the observable universe, detect neutron star mergers out to cosmological distances, precisely test general relativity in regimes currently inaccessible, and potentially discover unexpected sources. The computational challenges of handling tens of thousands of detections per year, extracting maximum information from each observation, and testing theoretical predictions with unprecedented precision will define much of the research landscape for the coming decades.

Additionally, pulsar timing arrays have recently reported evidence for a stochastic gravitational wave background at nanohertz frequencies, likely from supermassive black hole binaries across the universe. This opens yet another frequency window for gravitational wave observations, complementing ground-based and space-based detectors.

The gravitational wave revolution has moved from first detection to regular observations to planning for transformative new capabilities—all in less than a decade. The field is simultaneously harvesting the scientific returns from current detections and preparing for facilities that will observe the gravitational wave sky in exquisite detail. For researchers entering the field now, this creates a unique opportunity: contributing to both near-term challenges with current data and laying groundwork for the next generation of discoveries.

## 1.3 Structure of This Study

This feasibility study is organized around four fundamental questions that drive contemporary gravitational wave research:

**What are the fundamental properties of gravity and spacetime?** (Section 2)  
Gravitational waves provide our only tool for testing general relativity in the strong-field, highly dynamical regime where nonlinear effects dominate. This section examines how observations test GR's predictions, what higher-order modes and memory effects reveal about spacetime structure, and where computational challenges limit our ability to make predictions or test alternatives to GR.

**What is the nature and lifecycle of compact objects?** (Section 3)  
Black holes and neutron stars represent the endpoints of stellar evolution and the most extreme environments in the universe. Gravitational wave observations constrain their properties—masses, spins, multipole structure—and reveal how they form, evolve, and merge. This section covers both the astrophysics of compact objects and the computational challenges in modeling their dynamics, particularly for neutron stars where microphysics matters.

**How do we extract physics from gravitational wave observations?** (Section 4)  
Detecting gravitational waves is one challenge; understanding what they tell us is another. This section examines the full pipeline from waveform modeling (including numerical relativity's crucial role) through data analysis, parameter estimation, and inference. It covers traditional methods, emerging machine learning approaches, and the computational infrastructure required to handle increasing detection rates. Multi-messenger observations receive special attention for their unique scientific value.

**What does the future hold?** (Section 5)  
Current detectors represent just the beginning of gravitational wave astronomy. This section surveys the observational landscape today, describes next-generation ground-based and space-based facilities, and explores what new science they will enable. Particular attention is paid to the computational challenges that will accompany improved sensitivity, from simulating new source populations to handling vastly increased data volumes.

Each section follows a consistent approach: establishing the theoretical framework, identifying computational challenges and current capabilities, reviewing observational status and prospects, and explicitly highlighting open problems and opportunities for contribution. Cross-references throughout the document connect related topics—how theoretical questions translate into computational challenges, how computational capabilities enable new observational analyses, how observational requirements drive computational development.

Following these main sections, **Section 6 (Synthesis and Proposals)** draws together the threads from the entire study. It summarizes key challenges across theoretical, computational, and observational domains, identifies potential research directions ranging from near-term projects to ambitious multi-year efforts, and evaluates specific thesis topics based on scientific impact, computational feasibility, and connection to available resources and expertise.

**Section 7 (Conclusion)** returns to the three motivating questions: Why does gravitational wave physics matter? How relevant is it currently? What can be accomplished in this field? It provides final synthesis and perspective on the field's trajectory and opportunities for contribution.

The extensive reference list reflects both historical papers that established key results and recent work (2023-2025) that represents the current frontier. Appendices provide technical resources: a glossary of specialized terms, information about key collaborations and data access, and descriptions of computational tools and codes available to researchers.

This study aims to serve multiple purposes: as a comprehensive introduction to gravitational wave physics for someone with a solid physics background, as a reference for understanding connections between different subfields, and most importantly, as a foundation for identifying concrete, feasible research directions that could form the basis of a master's thesis. Throughout the document, computational aspects receive special emphasis, reflecting both the field's intrinsic dependence on large-scale computation and the particular research interests that motivate this study.

The gravitational wave frontier is vast, diverse, and rapidly evolving. This study maps that frontier, identifying both established landmarks and unexplored territories where meaningful contributions can still be made. Let us begin.
