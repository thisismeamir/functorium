# Regular Hausdorff Space

A topological space $X$ is a regular space if, given any closed set $F$ and any point $x$ that does not belong to $F$, there exists a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ and a neighborhood $V$ of $F$ that are disjoint. Concisely put, it must be possible to separate $x$ and $F$ with disjoint neighborhoods.

A $\mathrm{T}_{3}$ space or regular Hausdorff space is a topological space that is both regular and a Hausdorff space. It turns out that a space is $\mathrm{T}_{3}$ if and only if it is both regular and $\mathrm{T}_{0}$. Indeed, if a space is Hausdorff then it is $\mathrm{T}_{0}$ and each $\mathrm{T}_{0}$ regular space is Hausdorff: given two distinct points, at least one of them misses the closure of the other one, so (by regularity) there exists disjoint neighborhoods separating one point from the other.

A _locally regular space_ is a topological space where every point has an open neighbourhood that is regular. Every regular space is locally regular, but the converse is not true. A classical example of a locally regular space that is not regular is the bug-eyed line.
