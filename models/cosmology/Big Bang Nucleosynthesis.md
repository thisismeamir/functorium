# Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

## The Early Hot Universe

By extrapolating the scale factor evolution backwards, we can reconstruct conditions in the early universe. At sufficiently early times, the universe was so **hot and dense** that matter could not exist in the form of atoms or even nuclei — any structure that formed was immediately destroyed by high-energy photons and particle collisions.

## Nucleosynthesis

As the universe expanded and cooled below the **[[binding energy]]** of nuclei (~few MeV), nuclear reactions could proceed without immediate photodisintegration. Light nuclei formed and survived — a process known as **[[Big Bang Nucleosynthesis]]** (BBN).

The sequence goes roughly:

$$p + n \rightarrow D, \quad D + D \rightarrow {}^4\text{He}, \quad \ldots$$

Knowing the conditions of the early universe (temperature, density, expansion rate) and the relevant **[[nuclear cross-sections]]**, we can calculate the expected **[[primordial abundances]]** of light elements:

- **[[Hydrogen]]** (~75% by mass)
- **[[Helium-4]]** (~25% by mass)
- Trace amounts of **[[Deuterium]]**, **[[Helium-3]]**, and **[[Lithium-7]]**

These predicted abundances are in remarkable agreement with observations — one of the key pillars of the Big Bang paradigm.

> **Note:** BBN is sensitive to the **[[baryon-to-photon ratio]]** $\eta$, making primordial abundance measurements a powerful probe of early universe physics. The tension in observed ${}^7\text{Li}$ abundance vs. BBN predictions remains an open problem — the **[[Lithium problem]]**.
