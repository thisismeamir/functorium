## Differential Forms

### Exterior Derivative

What are differential forms? 
a $p$-form is a tensor of rank $(0,p)$ that is antisymmetric. With this definition
- Scalars are $0$-forms
- Dual vectors are $1$-forms
- And so on
For example the Electromagnetic tensor $F_{\mu \nu}$ is a $2$-form.

Forms help us with integration, and differentiations, as you might recall we've got troubles defining a derivative when we are dealing with curved spaces. We had two options in general to define a derivative. This was mainly due to the fact that there's no unique way to transport a vector from one tangent space to another tangent space. For this we've developed the covariant derivative.

$$
\nabla_{\mu}V^\nu
$$

And we also had a Lie derivative, defining a derivation with respect to a vector field.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{X}V
$$

But differential forms gives us a very suitable candidate to define a derivative, noting that it is only useful in some cases, we've defined $\mathrm{d}$ operator:

$$
(\mathrm{d}A)_{\mu_{1}\dots \mu_{p+1}} = (p+1)\partial_{[\mu_{1}}A_{\mu_{2}\dots \mu_{p+1}}
$$
This is called a exterior derivative, it is only defined for $p$-forms and not on general tensors, although the result is clean and is a tensor of rank $(0,p+1)$.

Exterior derivative has an interesting property which comes from topology: 
$$
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}A)=0 
$$
or more simply written:

$$
\mathrm{d}^{2} =0
$$

***The boundary of a boundary is zero***


$$
\text{if } \mathrm{d}A =0 \implies \text{Closed Form}
$$

$$
\text{if } A = \mathrm{d}B \implies \text{A is exact form}
$$

All forms that are exact are closed as well. but the reverse might not be true.

## Wedge Product

We define the Wedge product as an operator as follows:

$$
(A\wedge B)_{\mu_{1}\dots \mu_{p+q}} = \frac{(p+q)!}{p!q!} A_{[\mu_{1}\dots \mu_{p}}B_{\mu_{p+1}\dots \mu_{p+q}]}
$$

Wedge product in general gets a $p$-form and a $q$-form and gives $(p+q)$-form

Wedge product is some-what distributive with respect to the exterior derivative.

$$
\mathrm{d}(\omega \wedge \eta) = \mathrm{d}\omega \wedge \eta + (-1)^{p}\omega \wedge \mathrm{d}\eta 
$$

## Integration

Differential forms can help us define derivations, and integrations more clearly. 

### Gradient:

Gradient is an operator that operates on a $0$-form and returns a $1$-form, prove is left for us but we can show that

$$
\mathrm{d}f = (\partial_{i}f)\mathrm{d}x^{i}
$$
Which is the gradient defined as we know.

### Curl

As we know curl is an operator that given a $1$-form returns another $1$-form. It is obvious that we can show:

$$
\mathrm{d}\omega = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}A_{j}-\partial_{j}A_{i})\mathrm{d}x^{i}\wedge \mathrm{d}x^{j}
$$

Which is a $2$-form. And one can show that 

$$
\star \mathrm{d}\omega = \nabla \times \omega
$$
### Divergence:

Also we can show that:

$$
\star \mathrm{d}(\star \omega) = \nabla \cdot \omega
$$

### Integration

It is provable that integration is fundamentally related and defined using the differential forms. In general integration is:

$$
\int _{\Sigma}: \omega \to \mathbb{R}
$$
Showing this would prove many theorems all in once because the Stoke's theorem, alongside with many others (Gauss's theorem for example) is to be written in a single formula as:

$$
\int _{\Sigma} \mathrm{d}\omega = \int _{\partial \Sigma} \omega 
$$

This fundamentally gives us many interpretations:
1. In one dimension this is the fundamental theorem of calculus
2. In two dimensions this is gauss's theorem.

Let's look at some examples:

---
$$
\int _{C}f(x)\mathrm{d}x = \int _{C} \omega
$$

where

$$
\omega = \omega(x)\mathrm{d}x
$$

---
Another one:

$$
\int f(x)\mathrm{d}^nx
$$

for the differential part (assume in 2 dimension) it had been considered a differential surface area or a differential volume, which is safe to say we are speaking of an operator that took the vectors and returned a number (namely, a form).

This can be shown more correctly as:

$$
\int _{\Sigma}\omega 
$$
where:

$$
\omega = \omega(x)\mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\dots \wedge \mathrm{d}x^n
$$

## An Important thing in the Class

In curved spaces we've to take notice of something important, consider:

$$
\int f \mathrm{d}x^n
$$
where

$$
\mathrm{d}x^n = \mathrm{d}x^{1}\dots \mathrm{d}x^{n} =  \left| \frac{\partial x}{\partial x'} \right| \mathrm{d}x'^{1}\dots \mathrm{d}x'^{n} 
$$
This can be considered in terms of forms as well. We might try to write:

$$
\mathrm{d}x^1 \wedge\dots \wedge \mathrm{d}x^n
$$

But this is not generally tensors, contradictory to our later discussions we might have thought wedge product gives $n+1$ forms, but here this is not the case, since $\mathrm{d}x$ are not real tensors, they are somewhat tensor densities, they depend on the coordinates because THEY ARE COORDINATE FUNCTIONS THEMSELVES!

> Therefore, it is important to take notice of what is happening here.

We have to make a tensor out of this in order to be able to use it in all coordinates. Similar to how we made a tensor out of the Levi-Chevita symbol we have:

$$
 \sqrt{ \left| g \right|  } \mathrm{d}x^1 \wedge\dots \wedge \mathrm{d}x^{n} \equiv \sqrt{ \left| g \right|  } \mathrm{d}^nx
$$

Therefore we can refactor the integration as:

$$
I = \int f(x) \sqrt{ \left| g \right|  } \mathrm{d}^nx
$$

This is the important part of this class. It will be used later on as well therefore, we need to be able to use it and remember it.
