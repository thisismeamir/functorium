Before the dawn of modern physics, thinkers grappled with fundamental questions about the nature of reality, often relying on philosophical arguments and limited empirical data. The prevailing worldview was deeply intertwined with cosmology and a qualitative understanding of natural phenomena. *   **Pre-17th Century: Foundations & Early Ideas**
    *   [[Aristotelian Physics]] (until ~16th century) – For centuries, the framework for understanding motion, matter, and the cosmos was largely defined by Aristotle's philosophical system. This system emphasized teleology and a hierarchical view of the universe, profoundly shaping scientific inquiry.
        *   [[Notion of Place]] – Geocentric Universe, fixed Earth. – Central to Aristotelian cosmology was the belief in a geocentric (Earth-centered) universe. The Earth was considered stationary at the center, with all other celestial bodies revolving around it. This view dictated a specific understanding of "place" within the cosmos.
        *   [[Notion of Time]] - Cyclical, linked to celestial movements. –  Time itself was conceived as cyclical and intimately connected to the apparent motions of the Sun, Moon, and stars. The regular recurrence of these events provided a natural framework for measuring time's passage.
        *   [[Qualitative vs. Quantitative Description]] – Early scientific endeavors primarily focused on *qualitative* descriptions of phenomena—explaining *why* things happened rather than precisely quantifying their behavior through mathematical relationships.  The development of rigorous quantitative methods was yet to come.
    *   [[Early astronomical observations (e.g., Ptolemy's Almagest)]] – [[Geocentric models]] and the need for complex explanations of [[planetary motion]]. – The accumulation of astronomical data, exemplified by Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, revealed that celestial bodies did not move in perfectly circular paths as initially assumed. This necessitated increasingly intricate mathematical constructs (epicycles, deferents) to reconcile observations with the geocentric model.
    *   [[Static Electricity]] – Ancient observations of amber attraction. –  The phenomenon of static electricity was recognized even in antiquity, with accounts of amber attracting light objects when rubbed. While the underlying mechanism remained unknown, these early observations marked the first tentative steps toward understanding electrical phenomena.
    *   [[Magnetism]] – Early knowledge of lodestones and magnetic properties. – Similarly, the peculiar behavior of lodestones—naturally magnetized rocks—was observed long ago.  The attraction and repulsion between lodestones provided an intriguing puzzle for early investigators, foreshadowing later developments in electromagnetism.

The 17th century witnessed a profound shift in scientific thought, marked by the emergence of new experimental methods and mathematical frameworks that fundamentally challenged established Aristotelian views. This period laid the groundwork for what we now recognize as classical mechanics and set the stage for later developments in relativity.

*   **17th Century: The Newtonian Revolution & Initial Frame Considerations**
    *   [[Galileo Galilei]] (early 1600s) – Galileo’s meticulous observations and ingenious experiments revolutionized our understanding of motion, challenging Aristotelian notions of natural place and impetus.
        *   [[atoms/Experiments with falling bodies]] - Inertia, constant acceleration. –  Galileo's careful study of falling objects demonstrated that they accelerate uniformly under the influence of gravity, regardless of their mass (neglecting air resistance). This observation hinted at the concept of inertia—the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion.
        *   [[Law of Uniform Acceleration]] – Galileo formulated a mathematical relationship describing the constant acceleration due to gravity, providing a quantitative link between time and distance for falling objects.  This was a crucial step towards a more precise description of physical phenomena.
        *   [[Galilean Relativity]] – Thought experiments involving motion in a ship. (Crucially, assumes [[../classical-mechanics/atoms/Absolute Time]] and [[../classical-mechanics/atoms/Absolute Space]]). – Galileo introduced the concept of relativity through thought experiments involving observers moving at constant velocity relative to one another. While insightful, his formulation implicitly assumed the existence of absolute time and space—a crucial point that would later be challenged.
    *   [[Johannes Kepler]] (early 1600s) – Building upon Tycho Brahe’s meticulous astronomical data, Kepler formulated a set of laws describing planetary motion with unprecedented accuracy.
        *   [[Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion]] - Empirical description of planetary orbits. – Kepler’s three laws provided an empirical description of planetary orbits, replacing circular paths with ellipses and introducing the concept of varying orbital speed. These laws were remarkably successful in predicting planetary positions but lacked a fundamental physical explanation at the time.
    *   [[Isaac Newton]] (1687) – Newton's *Principia Mathematica* stands as one of the most significant achievements in scientific history, synthesizing Kepler’s laws and Galileo’s insights into a unified framework for understanding motion and gravity.
        *   *[[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica]]* – This monumental work presented Newton’s laws of motion and universal gravitation, revolutionizing our understanding of the cosmos.
            *   [[Laws of Motion]] (Inertia, F=ma, Action-Reaction). – Newton articulated three fundamental laws governing the behavior of objects: inertia (an object resists changes in its state of motion), *F = ma* (force equals mass times acceleration), and action-reaction (for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction).
            *   [[Universal Gravitation]] – Inverse square law. – Newton’s law of universal gravitation described the attractive force between any two objects with mass, proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance separating them. This explained Kepler's laws and unified celestial and terrestrial mechanics.
            *   [[../classical-mechanics/atoms/Absolute Time]] - Universal, independent of observers. Mathematical parameter *t*. – Newton conceived of time as an absolute quantity, flowing uniformly throughout the universe, independent of any observer or physical process. It was treated as a universal mathematical parameter (*t*) against which all events could be measured.
            *   [[../classical-mechanics/atoms/Absolute Space]] - A fixed and immutable background against which motion occurs. – Similarly, Newton envisioned space as a fixed and unchanging framework—an absolute space—against which the positions and motions of objects could be defined.
            *   [[The Concept of a "Force"]] - As an interaction between objects in absolute space. –  Newton’s formulation of force explicitly linked it to interactions occurring *within* this absolute space, further reinforcing its foundational role in his mechanics.
        *   [[Fluxions (Calculus)]] – Mathematical tools for describing motion. – To describe the continuous changes involved in motion, Newton developed calculus—a powerful mathematical tool that allowed him to precisely analyze rates of change and areas under curves.
    *   [[Christiaan Huygens]] (1673) – Huygens’ work on wave propagation introduced early considerations of relative motion within a physical phenomenon.
        *   [[Huygens’ Principle]] – Wave propagation, early considerations of relative motion in wave phenomena. –  Huygens' principle provided a geometrical construction for understanding the propagation of waves, implicitly involving transformations between different reference frames—a precursor to relativistic concepts.

The late 18th and early 19th centuries witnessed a period of intense refinement and expansion within the Newtonian framework, alongside burgeoning discoveries in electromagnetism that would eventually challenge its foundations. While Newton’s laws remained remarkably successful in explaining a vast range of phenomena, subtle inconsistencies and new observations prompted deeper investigations and alternative formulations.

*   **Late 18th & Early 19th Century: Challenges and Refinements within Newtonian Framework**
    *   [[Leonhard Euler]] (mid-1700s) – Euler’s contributions significantly advanced the mathematical tools available for describing motion, extending the work begun by Newton.
        *   [[Euler Works on Kinematics]] – Further development of mathematical descriptions of motion. – Euler's work provided more sophisticated methods for analyzing kinematic relationships—the description of motion without considering its causes—laying the groundwork for more advanced mechanical analyses.
    *   [[Jean le Rond d’Alembert]] (mid-1700s) – D’Alembert’s principle offered a different perspective on the relationship between forces and accelerations, providing an alternative approach to solving problems in mechanics.
        *   [[D’Alembert’s Principle]] - Relationship between forces and accelerations. – This principle reformulated the equations of motion by introducing "inertial forces," allowing for a more symmetrical treatment of applied and fictitious forces.
    *   [[Joseph-Louis Lagrange]] (late 1700s) – Lagrange's formulation of mechanics provided a more general and elegant framework than Newton’s, emphasizing energy principles and offering a powerful tool for solving complex mechanical problems.
        *   [[Lagrangian Mechanics]] – A more general formulation of Newtonian mechanics, emphasizing energy principles. – Lagrangian mechanics shifted the focus from forces to energies, providing a more abstract and versatile approach to describing motion. This formalism proved particularly useful in dealing with constrained systems.
    *   [[Charles-Augustin de Coulomb]] (1785) – [[Coulomb's Law]]: Force between charges. – Coulomb’s law quantified the force of attraction or repulsion between electric charges, establishing a fundamental relationship analogous to Newton’s law of gravitation.
    *   [[Alessandro Volta]] (1799) – [[The voltaic pile]] (early battery). – The invention of the voltaic pile marked a crucial step in harnessing electrical energy, providing a source of continuous current for experimentation and paving the way for further discoveries in electromagnetism.
    *   [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]] (early 1800s) – Laplace’s work demonstrated the power of Newtonian physics to explain large-scale phenomena, extending its reach beyond Earth's immediate vicinity.
        *   [[Nebular Hypothesis]] - Formation of the Solar System. Demonstrates application of Newtonian physics to large-scale phenomena. – Laplace’s nebular hypothesis proposed a mechanism for the formation of the solar system based on gravitational collapse and rotation, showcasing the universality of Newtonian principles.
    *   [[Augustin Fresnel]] (early 1800s) – Fresnel's work initially faced resistance but ultimately established light as a wave phenomenon, introducing complexities related to relative motion.
        *   [[Wave Theory of Light]] – Initial resistance, then acceptance of light as a wave phenomenon. –  Fresnel’s experiments provided compelling evidence for the wave nature of light, challenging Newton’s corpuscular theory and opening up new avenues of investigation in optics.
        *   [[Notion of Wave Propagation]] - Medium dependence and the concept of velocity relative to the medium. – Fresnel's work introduced the concept of a "luminiferous ether"—a hypothetical medium through which light waves were thought to propagate—and highlighted the importance of considering the relative velocity between the wave and its medium.
    *   [[Benjamin Franklin]] – [[Electrical charge duality]] (+/-). – Franklin’s conceptualization of electrical charges as positive and negative provided a framework for understanding electrostatic phenomena, analogous to gravitational attraction and repulsion.
    *   [[Hans Christian Ørsted]] (1820) – [[Relationship Between Electricity and Magnetism]]. – Ørsted's groundbreaking discovery that an electric current could deflect a compass needle revealed a fundamental connection between electricity and magnetism—a pivotal moment in the development of electromagnetism.
    *   [[André-Marie Ampère]] (1820s) – [[Ampère's Law]]: Force between current-carrying wires. – Ampère’s law quantified the force between current-carrying wires, providing a mathematical description of the magnetic interaction between electric currents.
    *   [[George Ohm]] (1827) – [[Ohm’s Law]]: Voltage, Current, Resistance relationship. – Ohm's law established a fundamental relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in electrical circuits, providing a quantitative framework for analyzing electrical phenomena.
    *   [[Joseph Henry]] (1830s) - Discovery of [[Electromagnetic Induction]] (concurrently with Faraday). – Henry’s independent discovery of electromagnetic induction demonstrated that changing magnetic fields could induce electric currents—a phenomenon with profound technological implications.
    *   [[Michael Faraday]] (1831) – [[Faraday's Law of Induction]]: Changing magnetic fields induce electric currents. – Faraday’s law of induction provided a quantitative description of the relationship between changing magnetic fields and induced electric currents, solidifying the connection between electricity and magnetism.
    *   [[Notion of Magnetic Field Lines]] - Visualizing magnetic forces. – The introduction of magnetic field lines offered a powerful visual tool for understanding and analyzing magnetic forces and their interactions.

The mid-19th century witnessed a fervent pursuit to reconcile the burgeoning understanding of electromagnetism with the prevailing Newtonian framework, which relied heavily on the concept of a luminiferous ether—a hypothetical medium permeating all space and serving as the carrier for light waves. Scientists attempted to detect absolute motion through Earth's movement relative to this supposed ether, leading to increasingly perplexing experimental results that would ultimately challenge the foundations of classical physics.

- **Mid-19th Century: The Ether & Attempts at Absolute Motion Detection**
	-   [[Franz Neumann]] (1840s) – Early attempts to mathematically describe electromagnetic phenomena.
	    The investigations into electromagnetism were rapidly evolving, and Franz Neumann made early contributions towards a mathematical description of these forces, attempting to formulate equations governing their behavior. His work laid some groundwork for later, more comprehensive formulations.
	-   [[George Gabriel Stokes]] (1840s)
	    Stokes' contributions focused on refining the theoretical understanding of light and its interaction with matter within the context of the ether framework. He sought to reconcile experimental observations with the existing models.
	    *   [[Stokes’ Hypothesis]] - A model for how the [[atoms/Luminiferous Ether|Luminiferous Ether]] would interact with matter.
	        Stokes proposed a hypothesis detailing how the luminiferous ether, if it existed, would interact with matter, attempting to explain phenomena like refraction and dispersion through its interaction with the material medium. This was an attempt to provide a physical mechanism for these optical effects within the established ether model.
	-   [[Armand Hippolyte Louis Fizeau]] (1850)
	    Fizeau's experimental work aimed at directly measuring the speed of light, a crucial parameter in understanding its nature and potential connection to an absolute frame of reference.
	    *   [[Fizeau’s Experiment]] – First experimental attempt to measure the [[Speed of Light]], leading to an underestimation due to the "Ether wind" effect.
	        Fizeau devised an ingenious rotating-toothed wheel apparatus to measure the speed of light, obtaining a value that was significantly lower than later measurements. This initial underestimation was attributed to what became known as the “ether wind” – the expected relative motion between Earth and the stationary ether.
	-   [[Léon Foucault]] (1862)
	    Foucault refined the experimental approach for measuring the speed of light, employing a rotating mirror apparatus that provided more accurate results than previous attempts.
	    *   [[Foucault's Rotating Mirror Experiment]] – More accurate measurement of the speed of light, further complicating the Ether picture.
	        Foucault’s experiment yielded a higher value for the speed of light compared to Fizeau’s result, but crucially, it still failed to detect the expected “ether wind.” This discrepancy deepened the mystery surrounding the ether and its relationship to Earth's motion.
	-   [[James Clerk Maxwell]] (1865)
	    Maxwell’s work represented a monumental leap in understanding electromagnetism, unifying electric and magnetic phenomena into a single theoretical framework.
	    *   [[Maxwell's Equations]] – [[Unification of Electricity and Magnetism]], prediction of [[Electromagnetic Waves]] traveling at a constant speed $c$.
	        Maxwell formulated a set of equations that elegantly described the behavior of electric and magnetic fields, predicting the existence of electromagnetic waves propagating at a specific velocity—the speed of light. This profound connection suggested that light itself *was* an electromagnetic wave.
	    *   [[The Ether as the Medium for Electromagnetic Waves]] - A natural consequence of Maxwell’s equations. The concept of [[atoms/Luminiferous Ether|Luminiferous Ether]].
	        Within the prevailing paradigm, Maxwell's equations seemed to necessitate a medium—the luminiferous ether—to support the propagation of these electromagnetic waves, mirroring the role of air in sound transmission. This reinforced the belief in the existence and importance of the ether.
	    *   [[Notion of Field]] - Electric and Magnetic fields as fundamental entities.
	        Maxwell’s work shifted the conceptualization of electric and magnetic phenomena from being interactions between charges to being manifestations of underlying fields—regions of space exerting forces on charged particles. This introduced a more abstract and powerful way of describing these interactions.
	    *   [[Gauss’s Law for Electricity]]
	    *   [[Gauss’s Law for Magnetism]]
	    *   [[Faraday’s Law of Induction]] (Maxwell-Ampère form)
	    *   [[Ampère’s Law]] (Maxwell's addition of displacement current)
	-   [[Notion of Displacement Current]] - Maxwell's crucial addition to Ampere's law.
	    To ensure the consistency of his equations, Maxwell introduced the concept of "displacement current"—a fictitious current arising from changing electric fields—to modify Ampère’s Law. This seemingly minor modification had profound consequences for the theory.
	-   [[Prediction of Electromagnetic Waves]] – Derived from Maxwell’s Equations.
	    The combination of these equations allowed Maxwell to predict the existence and properties of electromagnetic waves, a revolutionary concept that fundamentally altered our understanding of light.
	-   [[Speed of Light]] (*c*) – Calculated from permittivity and permeability of free space.
	    Maxwell calculated the speed of these predicted electromagnetic waves using fundamental constants—the permittivity and permeability of free space—and found it to be remarkably close to the experimentally measured speed of light, solidifying the connection between electromagnetism and optics.

The late 19th century was dominated by the "ether drift controversy"—a period of intense theoretical and experimental investigation centered on detecting Earth's motion through the luminiferous ether. Despite increasingly sophisticated experiments, attempts to measure this “ether wind” consistently yielded null results, leading to a profound crisis in physics and ultimately paving the way for Einstein’s revolutionary theories. The prevailing explanations involved increasingly complex ad-hoc modifications of Newtonian mechanics and electromagnetism, attempting to reconcile theory with observation while clinging to the ether concept.

*   **Late 19th Century: The Ether Drift Controversy & Michelson-Morley Experiment**
	-   [[Oliver Heaviside]] (1880)
	    Heaviside made significant contributions to simplifying and clarifying Maxwell's equations, transforming them into a more compact and usable form for calculations and further theoretical development.
	    *   [[Electromagnetic Theory]] – Further development and simplification of Maxwell's Equations.
	        Heaviside’s work involved reorganizing and streamlining Maxwell’s equations, introducing vector notation that greatly facilitated their manipulation and application to various physical problems. This made the theory more accessible and fostered further advancements in electromagnetism.	
	-   [[Hendrik Lorentz]] (1886, 1892, 1895)
	    Lorentz played a pivotal role in attempting to explain the null results of ether drift experiments while maintaining a commitment to Newtonian mechanics. His mathematical formulations introduced concepts that would later be reinterpreted within the context of special relativity.
	    *   [[Lorentz Transformations]] (initial form) - Mathematical transformations to explain negative Ether drift results while preserving Newtonian mechanics.
	        To account for the absence of an ether wind, Lorentz developed a set of mathematical transformations—the initial form of what are now known as Lorentz transformations—that altered measurements of length and time depending on one's motion relative to the hypothetical ether frame. These transformations were initially conceived as a way to reconcile theory with experiment within the existing framework.
	    *   [[Local Lorentz Invariance]] – The idea that physical laws appear the same in all inertial frames *within* the Ether.
	        Lorentz proposed that physical laws would appear identical in all inertial frames moving uniformly relative to the ether—a concept known as local Lorentz invariance. This was an attempt to explain why experiments failed to detect any absolute motion through the ether; observers within different "ether frames" would experience the same physics.
	    *   [[Length Contraction and Time Dilation]] (as mathematical artifacts, not physical reality).
	        The Lorentz transformations predicted phenomena like length contraction and time dilation—effects where objects appear shorter and time passes slower in moving frames relative to a stationary ether frame. However, Lorentz viewed these as purely mathematical consequences of the transformation, rather than representing any actual physical changes.
	-   [[Henri Poincaré]] (1892, 1895)
	    Poincaré independently developed ideas remarkably similar to those of Lorentz, further emphasizing the relativity of simultaneity and the lack of a need for an absolute frame of reference.
	    *   [[Relative Ether Hypothesis]] - Similar to Lorentz's ideas, emphasizing the relativity of simultaneity and the lack of absolute frame.
	        Poincaré championed the idea that the laws of physics should be independent of one’s chosen reference frame—a concept known as the relative ether hypothesis. He recognized that the apparent absence of an ether wind could be explained by acknowledging the relativity of simultaneity, a crucial element in understanding how different observers perceive events.
	    *   [[Convention Hypothèse]] – The principle that physical laws should be independent of arbitrary conventions.
	        Poincaré articulated the "convention hypothesis," arguing that physical laws should remain unchanged regardless of the arbitrary choices made in defining measurement systems or coordinate frames. This underscored the importance of relativity and challenged the notion of an absolute, privileged frame of reference.
	-   [[Albert Michelson]] & [[Edward Morley]] (1887)
	    Michelson and Morley conducted a landmark experiment designed to definitively detect Earth's motion through the ether. The results were profoundly disappointing.
	    *   [[Michelson-Morley Experiment]] – Null result for Ether drift, considered a major crisis in physics.
	        The Michelson-Morley experiment, utilizing an interferometer to measure tiny shifts in light interference patterns due to Earth’s supposed motion through the ether, yielded a null result—no detectable ether wind was found. This outcome presented a severe challenge to the established theoretical framework and sparked intense debate within the scientific community.
	    *   [[The Problem of Simultaneity]] - How to define simultaneity without an absolute frame of reference.
	        The Michelson-Morley experiment highlighted the fundamental problem of defining simultaneity—the concept of events occurring at the same time—without a universally agreed-upon, absolute frame of reference. The lack of such a frame made it difficult to interpret experimental results and reconcile them with theoretical predictions.
	-   [[James Clerk Maxwell]] (1867) – On Physical Lines of Force - Further development of field theory.
	    Maxwell continued to refine his understanding of electromagnetism, expanding on the concept of fields as fundamental entities in describing physical interactions.
	-   [[Heinrich Hertz]] (1880s) – Experimental verification of electromagnetic waves, including radio waves.
	    Hertz’s experiments provided crucial experimental confirmation of Maxwell's theoretical predictions regarding the existence and properties of electromagnetic waves, including the generation and detection of radio waves. This solidified the connection between electromagnetism and light.
	-   [[The Ether and Electromagnetic Waves]] – The need for a medium to propagate EM waves.
	    Despite mounting evidence to the contrary, the prevailing view remained that electromagnetic waves, like all other known wave phenomena, required a medium—the ether—to propagate. This belief strongly influenced theoretical interpretations and experimental designs.
	-   [[Notion of Permittivity & Permeability]] - Properties of the medium supporting wave propagation.
	    Scientists continued to investigate the properties of the hypothetical ether, focusing on parameters like permittivity and permeability—quantities that determined how electric and magnetic fields behaved within the medium and influenced the speed of electromagnetic waves.

## Michelson and Morley Experiment

The success of Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism made the ether as such a central object of study and debate in late nineteenth-century physics. There was considerable pressure on experiments to try to observe it directly. In 1881 Michelson and Morley decided to put to the test the [[atoms/Luminiferous Ether]] hypotheses.

They split a bean of light and sent it along orthogonal paths of equal length and back again, whereupon interference fringes were produced between the returning beams. Different ether wind components along the two paths should have led to a difference in travel times. However, when the apparatus was rotated through $90^\degree$, so that this difference should be reversed, the expected displacement of the fringes did not occur.

By calculating some numbers one would understand that the effects could be perceived (if existed) through the apparatus. The most obvious defense is that the earth is dragging with ether itself, which can be ruled out because of the various optical phenomena that has been observed.  

## Lorentz

An ingenious explanation of the Michelson and Morley Experiment null results was found by Fitz Gerald in 1889. He suggested that the lengths of bodies moving through ether at velocity $v$ contract in the direction of their motion by a factor 

$$
\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}
$$

which would just compensate for the ether drift in the Michelson-Morley apparatus. Three years later Lorentz made the same hypothesis and incorporate it into his even more comprehensive ether theory.
